# Dreaming and consciousness: a scoped reference list

Status: exploratory literature review. This is a separate research track, not a change to agent training or the dark room roadmap.

## Scope

Question: does internally generated activity develop or reveal consciousness-related functions beyond its benefits for learning and memory?

This document records an initial set of sources and their evidence limits. It is not a systematic review. It does not establish that dreaming causes consciousness, that artificial agents dream, or that the project agent is conscious.

No experiment, training change, new indicator score, or architecture change follows from this review. Any later experiment needs its own proposal and approval. Experimental outputs and weights must remain separate from the main training track.

## Terms and inference limits

- Dream experience: subjective experience during sleep. A report provides evidence about it, not direct access to it.
- Dream report: a description affected by experience, memory, language, and the interview method.
- Internal simulation: generated states without corresponding new sensory observations. This is a computational capability, not proof of experience.
- Replay: reuse or reactivation of prior information. Replay need not be a dream.
- Perceptual hallucination: an experience resembling perception without the corresponding external stimulus. An incorrect AI answer is a different research target.
- Substrate agnostic: leave open whether consciousness requires biology.
- Substrate independent: propose that relevant causal organization can support consciousness in different physical materials. This remains a theoretical position, not a result established by this review.

Three claims need separate evidence:

1. Internal simulation improves learning.
2. Internal simulation develops functions identified by theories of consciousness.
3. A system experiences its internally generated content.

Evidence for one claim does not establish the next. Absence of a dream report does not establish absence of experience. Reflective awareness and narrative competence must not be treated as equivalent to all conscious experience.

## Reference list

Access labels describe material checked during preparation. Full text available online does not mean that its methods were checked. Bibliographic links identify sources; they do not imply endorsement of every claim in those sources.

### Development and competing accounts

**1. Foulkes, D. (1999). _Children's Dreaming and the Development of Consciousness_. Harvard University Press.**

Source: https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1999-02151-000

- Contribution: links development of narrative dreaming with waking cognitive skills and reflective awareness.
- Evidence limit: laboratory dream reports cannot fully separate experience from recall and reporting. This is not a timetable for the onset of all consciousness.
- Interpretation limit: the proposed direction is that cognitive development enables dreaming, not that dreaming creates consciousness.
- Access: publication record and publisher description checked. Book passages supplied through a reader's NotebookLM summary remain unverified against the book. No page quotations or numerical findings from that summary are reproduced here.

**2. Sándor, P., Szakadát, S., Kertész, K., and Bódizs, R. (2015). Content analysis of 4 to 8 year-old children's dream reports. _Frontiers in Psychology_, 6, 534.**

Source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00534/full

- Evidence: parent-collected home reports include active dreamers, movement, social interaction, and emotion in preschool children.
- Relevance: these reports challenge a simple developmental sequence inferred from sparse laboratory reports.
- Evidence limit: home reports lack physiological sleep staging. Recall delay, parental influence, and reconstruction remain possible explanations. The study does not isolate the causal effect of collection setting.
- Access: full article checked.

**3. Hobson, J. A. (2009). REM sleep and dreaming: towards a theory of protoconsciousness. _Nature Reviews Neuroscience_, 10, 803-813.**

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/nrn2716

- Proposal: REM activity helps develop and maintain systems needed for waking consciousness.
- Relevance: supplies a possible developmental function for internally generated activity.
- Evidence limit: this is a review and theory article. A developmental function for REM physiology does not establish that experienced dreams perform that function. REM is not equivalent to dreaming.
- Access: publisher abstract, key points, and references checked. Full text not reviewed.

### Dreaming, sleep state, and measurement

**4. Solms, M. (2000). Dreaming and REM sleep are controlled by different brain mechanisms. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, 23(6), 843-850.**

Source: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X00003988

- Evidence described: clinical and physiological observations separate reported dreaming from REM sleep.
- Relevance: argues against using REM physiology as a complete substitute for dream experience.
- Evidence limit: lesions can also affect memory, imagery, and communication. Detailed patient methods and recall controls need full-text review.
- Access: publisher abstract and bibliographic record checked. Full text not reviewed. The issue year is 2000; online publication was later.

**5. Siclari, F., et al. (2017). The neural correlates of dreaming. _Nature Neuroscience_, 20, 872-878.**

Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5462120/

- Evidence: EEG activity before awakenings differs with subsequent reports of experience and dream content in REM and NREM sleep.
- Measurement strength: distinguishes reported experience with content recall, reported experience without content recall, and reported absence of experience.
- Evidence limit: predicting a report is not direct access to experience. Neural association does not establish that one region alone causes consciousness. Human EEG measures and thresholds do not automatically transfer to software.
- Access: full author manuscript checked.

**6. Windt, J. M., Nielsen, T., and Thompson, E. (2016). Does Consciousness Disappear in Dreamless Sleep? _Trends in Cognitive Sciences_, 20(12), 871-882.**

Source: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.09.006

- Proposal: some sleep experience may lack the features of a narrative dream.
- Relevance: challenges the assumption that dreamless sleep is necessarily unconscious.
- Evidence limit: this review and conceptual proposal does not establish continuous consciousness throughout sleep. Lack of recalled content remains ambiguous.
- Access: abstract and bibliographic record checked. Full text not reviewed.

**7. Konkoly, K. R., et al. (2021). Real-time dialogue between experimenters and dreamers during REM sleep. _Current Biology_, 31(7), 1417-1427.e6.**

Source: https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC8162929

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2021.01.026

- Evidence: some participants in verified REM sleep answered questions using trained eye or facial signals.
- Relevance: provides a way to study cognition during lucid dreams without relying only on later narrative recall.
- Evidence limit: selected successful communication during lucid dreams does not describe all dreaming. It does not show that dreaming creates consciousness or supplies a consciousness test for AI.
- Access: full-text XML checked, including methods. Not every section was inspected in detail.

### Learning, replay, and hallucination

**8. Wamsley, E. J., Tucker, M., Payne, J. D., Benavides, J. A., and Stickgold, R. (2010). Dreaming of a learning task is associated with enhanced sleep-dependent memory consolidation. _Current Biology_, 20(9), 850-855.**

Source: https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC2869395

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2010.03.027

- Evidence: task-related dream reports after virtual maze learning were associated with later performance improvement.
- Relevance: connects reported dream content with offline memory processing.
- Evidence limit: association does not show that experiencing the dream caused improvement. A shared memory process could support both. The study does not test consciousness development.
- Access: abstract and metadata checked. Full-text retrieval failed, so methods beyond the abstract remain unverified.

**9. Girardeau, G., Benchenane, K., Wiener, S. I., Buzsáki, G., and Zugaro, M. B. (2009). Selective suppression of hippocampal ripples impairs spatial memory. _Nature Neuroscience_, 12(10), 1222-1223.**

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/nn.2384

- Evidence: selective disruption of hippocampal ripple events after learning impaired spatial memory in rats.
- Relevance: provides intervention evidence about physiology associated with offline memory processing, beyond a correlation with reports.
- Evidence limit: ripple disruption is not selective removal of dream experience or replay content. There are no dream reports or consciousness measures. This cannot establish a causal role for dreams in consciousness.
- Access: abstract and figure captions checked. Full text not reviewed.

**10. Powers, A. R., Mathys, C., and Corlett, P. R. (2017). Pavlovian conditioning-induced hallucinations result from overweighting of perceptual priors. _Science_, 357(6351), 596-600.**

Source: https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC5802347

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aan3458

- Evidence: conditioning, computational modeling, and brain imaging connect reports of absent tones with perceptual expectations.
- Relevance: provides a distinct research approach to how internal expectations affect perceptual reports.
- Evidence limit: this concerns waking human perception. Model-based explanations do not establish that similar calculations in software produce experience. The study does not test dreaming or consciousness development.
- Access: abstract and bibliographic record checked. Full text not reviewed.

**11. Hoel, E. (2020). The Overfitted Brain: Dreams evolved to assist generalization. arXiv preprint, 2007.09560, version 2.**

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.09560v2

- Proposal: unusual generated experiences reduce overfitting and help generalization.
- Relevance: connects a proposed biological function of dreams with machine learning concepts.
- Evidence limit: this entry cites the preprint, not a separately verified journal version. A learning benefit from generated inputs would not demonstrate experienced dreams or consciousness.
- Access: preprint abstract and version metadata checked. Full text not reviewed.

### Artificial simulation and consciousness across substrates

**12. Ha, D., and Schmidhuber, J. (2018). World Models. arXiv preprint, 1803.10122.**

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.10122

- Evidence described: a policy can learn inside a generated internal environment and transfer to the original environment.
- Relevance: establishes a concrete computational meaning for training in a model-generated environment.
- Evidence limit: the term dream describes generated training states. It is not evidence of subjective experience. Functional task success does not establish substrate independence of consciousness.
- Access: abstract and version metadata checked. Full methods not reviewed.

**13. Butlin, P., et al. (2023). Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Science of Consciousness. arXiv report, 2308.08708.**

Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708

- Contribution: derives computational indicator properties from several scientific theories of consciousness.
- Relevance: supplies a framework for asking which functions might matter in artificial systems, rather than relying on dream-like outputs alone.
- Evidence limit: indicator coverage is not a decisive test of consciousness. Satisfying a proposed computation does not settle whether physical substrate matters. The report's assessment of systems available at publication is not a current assessment of all AI.
- Access: abstract and version metadata checked for this review. Full theoretical arguments need separate review before adopting them here.

**14. Deperrois, N., Petrovici, M. A., Senn, W., and Jordan, J. (2022). Learning cortical representations through perturbed and adversarial dreaming. _eLife_, 11, e76384.**

Source: https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.76384

- Evidence described: a computational model uses wake, perturbed replay, and adversarial generation phases to learn image representations.
- Relevance: supplies a closer computational comparison to proposed sleep functions than dream terminology alone.
- Evidence limit: performance on image tasks tests representation learning. It does not establish dream experience or the emergence of consciousness. Biological phase labels do not establish physiological equivalence.
- Access: metadata and abstract checked through Europe PMC. Full methods and ablations not reviewed.

**15. Chalmers, D. J. (1995). Absent Qualia, Fading Qualia, Dancing Qualia. In T. Metzinger (Ed.), _Conscious Experience_. Imprint Academic.**

Source: https://consc.net/papers/qualia.html

- Argument: systems with the same sufficiently detailed causal organization, in corresponding states, have the same experience. Neural replacement thought experiments support this proposed organizational invariance.
- Relevance: states an explicit assumption under which consciousness could occur in different materials.
- Evidence limit: Chalmers calls the argument a plausibility argument, not conclusive proof. Matching task performance alone does not demonstrate the required causal organization. This is philosophy, not an experiment on artificial dream experience.
- Access: author-hosted text checked, including the definition of organizational invariance and stated limitations.

**16. Searle, J. R. (1980). Minds, brains, and programs. _Behavioral and Brain Sciences_, 3(3), target article 417-424.**

Source: https://home.csulb.edu/~cwallis/382/readings/482/searle.minds.brains.programs.bbs.1980.pdf

- Argument: implementing a formal program is not by itself sufficient for understanding or intentionality. An artificial system would need the relevant causal powers, rather than only a formal simulation.
- Relevance: challenges the inference from computational reproduction to mental states. Intentionality, meaning mental directedness toward something, is not identical to subjective experience.
- Evidence limit: this is a disputed philosophical argument, not an empirical demonstration that artificial consciousness is impossible. Searle explicitly leaves open whether other physical or chemical processes could produce the relevant effects. Do not reduce the argument to a claim that only carbon can think.
- Access: readable text extracted from a university-hosted scan; abstract and target-article argument checked. The scan includes commentaries that are not reviewed here. OCR contains errors, so no verbatim quotations are used.

**17. Rouleau, N., and Levin, M. (2023). The Multiple Realizability of Sentience in Living Systems and Beyond. _eNeuro_, 10(11), ENEURO.0375-23.2023.**

Source: https://www.eneuro.org/content/10/11/ENEURO.0375-23.2023

- Argument: study cognitive functions across conventional and unconventional physical systems instead of assuming that only brains can implement them.
- Relevance: supports broader candidate substrates and explicit, consistently applied operational definitions.
- Evidence limit: this is a commentary. The authors recognize that subjective experience remains inferred and that the same functional outcomes can occur without sentience. Multiple implementations of learning do not by themselves establish multiple implementations of experience.
- Access: commentary text checked, including the sections on inference limits and embodied systems.

### Substrate comparison

The relevant disagreement is about which physical and causal properties are sufficient for experience, not whether a system has a label such as dreamer.

| Position | Research implication | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Organizational invariance, Chalmers [15] | Specify the causal organization and corresponding state that a different implementation must preserve. | Matching outputs or broad module names is insufficient. The principle remains argued, not experimentally established here. |
| Causal powers objection, Searle [16] | Ask whether the implementation has the relevant powers rather than merely describes them. | The argument does not identify a complete empirical test or rule out every artificial material. |
| Multiple realizability, Rouleau and Levin [17] | Compare functions across diverse systems using consistent definitions. | Cognitive performance is not a direct measurement of sentience. |
| Computational indicators, Butlin et al. [13] | State theory-specific requirements before interpreting an artificial system. | No indicator supplies a theory-neutral proof of experience. |

A computational dream model [14] can test a proposed learning mechanism without settling these disagreements. If artificial dream experience were established independently, it would support experience beyond biological brains. A separate intervention would still be needed to show that dreaming caused its development. Internal generation alone establishes neither conclusion.

## Current synthesis

The reviewed sources support a research connection, not a single causal chain.

| Question | What these sources support | What remains unresolved |
|---|---|---|
| Does dream reporting change with development? | Developmental differences and disagreement across collection methods. | How much reflects changes in experience rather than memory, language, or interviews. |
| Can sleep include consciousness? | Reports, neural associations, and communication during some lucid dreams. | A complete measure of experience, including cases without report. |
| Does offline activity contribute to memory? | Behavioral associations and causal physiological interventions. | Whether experienced dreaming, rather than associated processing, supplies the benefit. |
| Can artificial systems learn through internal simulation? | Computational work describes learning in generated environments. | Whether any such system has subjective experience. |
| Does dreaming create consciousness? | Relevant proposals and possible mechanisms. | No reviewed source establishes this causal claim. |
| Can consciousness transfer across substrates? | Computational indicators provide candidate functional descriptions. | This list does not establish substrate independence or adequately cover its objections. |

The relationship could have several forms: cognitive development could enable richer dreaming; internally generated activity could help learning; shared mechanisms could support both. These explanations can coexist. Their relative roles remain questions for research.

## Candidate questions, not approved experiments

- Which developmental findings survive different reporting methods?
- Does internally generated content reveal a capacity that observation-driven tasks do not measure?
- Can an intervention separate simulation, memory benefit, and functions proposed by consciousness theories?
- What control would distinguish structured internal generation from memorized replay, random activity, or decoder bias?
- Which functional claims survive changes in physical implementation, and which theories reject that transfer?

A future computational study must define its target before selecting a measure. A measure of prediction, retention, or generalization must retain that name. It must not become a consciousness score by relabeling.

## Review gaps and next decisions

1. Verify the Foulkes passages against the book and trace claims to the original studies.
2. Read full methods for sources currently supported only by abstracts.
3. Extend the substrate comparison to current biological objections and the original Rouleau and Levin review of unconventional embodiments. Selected arguments above do not exhaust the debate.
4. Separate developmental evidence from adult state comparisons and animal memory studies.
5. Check later replications and challenges before describing any result as established.
6. Decide whether the evidence warrants a narrower theory proposal. Do not claim novelty from this initial list.
